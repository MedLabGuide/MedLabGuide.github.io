Uric Acid Blood Test: Understanding Gout and Hyperuricemia

What is Uric Acid and Why is it Tested?

Uric acid is the end product of purine metabolism in the human body. Purines are natural substances found in all body cells and in many foods. When cells break down or when you digest purine-rich foods, the purines are converted to uric acid.

Normally, uric acid dissolves in blood, passes through the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. Problems arise when the body produces too much uric acid or the kidneys excrete too little, causing uric acid to accumulate in the blood (hyperuricemia).

Chronic hyperuricemia leads to urate crystal deposition in joints (gout), kidneys (uric acid kidney stones), and other tissues.

Uric Acid Normal Range

Normal uric acid levels:

- Men: 3.4-7.0 mg/dL (202-416 μmol/L)

- Women: 2.4-6.0 mg/dL (143-357 μmol/L)

- Children: 2.0-5.5 mg/dL

Hyperuricemia is defined as uric acid above 7.0 mg/dL in men and 6.0 mg/dL in women.

The lower target for gout treatment is typically below 6.0 mg/dL (360 μmol/L) — at this level, urate crystals gradually dissolve. For patients with tophi (visible crystal deposits), the target is below 5.0 mg/dL.

Causes of High Uric Acid

Dietary causes:

- High purine foods: red meat, organ meats, shellfish (especially shrimp, lobster)

- Alcohol (especially beer — contains yeast with high purines; alcohol also reduces uric acid excretion)

- Fructose-sweetened beverages (corn syrup sodas)

Medical causes:

- Obesity and metabolic syndrome

- Hypertension

- Chronic kidney disease (reduced excretion)

- Hypothyroidism

- Psoriasis (high cell turnover)

- Certain cancers and chemotherapy (rapid cell destruction)

Medications:

- Diuretics (thiazides, furosemide) — most common drug cause

- Low-dose aspirin

- Cyclosporine (immunosuppressant)

- Niacin (vitamin B3 in high doses)

Gout — Symptoms and Diagnosis

Gout is the most common manifestation of hyperuricemia, characterized by sudden, severe joint pain caused by urate crystal deposition.

Classic presentation:

- Sudden onset of intense joint pain, typically at night

- Most commonly affects the big toe (podagra) — in 50-60% of first attacks

- Also affects ankle, knee, wrist, elbow

- Joint becomes red, swollen, warm, exquisitely tender (even bedsheet touching causes pain)

- Attack resolves in 7-14 days without treatment

Important: Serum uric acid may be NORMAL or even LOW during an acute gout attack (urate crystals leave the blood as they deposit in joints). Do not use serum uric acid alone to diagnose acute gout — diagnosis is confirmed by joint fluid analysis showing needle-shaped urate crystals.

Treatment and Prevention

Acute gout attack:

- NSAIDs (indomethacin, naproxen) — first-line if no contraindications

- Colchicine — very effective if started within 24 hours

- Corticosteroids — for those who cannot take NSAIDs or colchicine

Long-term urate-lowering therapy (after 2+ attacks):

- Allopurinol — first-line, reduces uric acid production

- Febuxostat — alternative to allopurinol

- Probenecid — increases renal excretion (less used)

Dietary changes:

- Reduce red meat, organ meats, shellfish

- Avoid alcohol, especially beer

- Eliminate fructose-sweetened drinks

- Increase low-fat dairy (actually protective)

- Stay well hydrated (2-3L water daily dilutes urine and helps excretion)

- Cherries and cherry extract may reduce attack frequency

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Do I need to fast before this test?

Fasting requirements vary by test. Always check with your doctor or the laboratory about specific preparation instructions for your ordered tests.

Q: How long does it take to get results?

Most routine blood tests return results within 24-48 hours. Urgent tests may be available in hours. Specialized tests may take several days to weeks.

Q: What should I do if my result is abnormal?

Contact your doctor to discuss any abnormal results. Do not self-diagnose or adjust medications based on laboratory results alone — your doctor will interpret results in the context of your complete clinical picture.
