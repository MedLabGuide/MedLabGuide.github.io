TSH Blood Test Explained: What Your Thyroid Results Really Mean

What is TSH and Why is it Tested?

TSH stands for Thyroid Stimulating Hormone, a hormone produced by the pituitary gland (a small gland at the base of your brain). TSH's job is to tell your thyroid gland how much thyroid hormone to produce.

The TSH test is the single best screening test for thyroid dysfunction. It's included in many routine health check-ups and is particularly important for:

- Women over 35 (thyroid problems are 5-8 times more common in women)

- Anyone with symptoms of thyroid problems

- People with a family history of thyroid disease

- Pregnant women and those planning pregnancy

- Patients on thyroid medication (to monitor treatment)

TSH Normal Range — Understanding Your Results

The normal TSH range varies slightly between laboratories, but generally:

Standard adult normal range: 0.4 – 4.0 mIU/L

Optimal range for most adults: 0.5 – 2.5 mIU/L

Pregnant women (first trimester): 0.1 – 2.5 mIU/L

Pregnant women (second trimester): 0.2 – 3.0 mIU/L

Adults over 60: Upper limit may be slightly higher (up to 6.0 mIU/L)

The key principle: TSH works in reverse to thyroid hormone levels:

- High TSH = thyroid isn't making enough hormone (hypothyroidism)

- Low TSH = thyroid is making too much hormone (hyperthyroidism)

Symptoms of Hypothyroidism (High TSH)

When TSH is high, your thyroid is underactive (hypothyroidism). Common symptoms include:

- Fatigue and sluggishness, even after adequate sleep

- Weight gain despite no change in diet

- Feeling cold when others feel comfortable

- Dry, flaky skin and brittle nails

- Hair thinning or loss

- Constipation

- Depression or low mood

- Brain fog, difficulty concentrating

- Slow heart rate

- Muscle weakness and joint pain

- Heavy or irregular menstrual periods (in women)

Many people have several of these symptoms for years before diagnosis, because hypothyroidism develops very gradually.

Symptoms of Hyperthyroidism (Low TSH)

When TSH is low, your thyroid is overactive (hyperthyroidism). Common symptoms include:

- Unintentional weight loss despite increased appetite

- Rapid or irregular heartbeat (palpitations)

- Anxiety, nervousness, or irritability

- Trembling hands or fingers

- Excessive sweating and heat intolerance

- Frequent bowel movements or diarrhea

- Sleep difficulties

- Enlarged thyroid gland (goiter) visible in neck

- Muscle weakness

- Light or missed menstrual periods (in women)

- Protruding eyes (in Graves' disease specifically)

What to Do If Your TSH is Abnormal

If TSH is high (hypothyroidism):

Your doctor will likely order Free T4 and possibly TPO antibodies to confirm the diagnosis. Treatment is usually levothyroxine (synthetic T4 hormone) — a once-daily tablet that most patients take for life. Dose is adjusted based on follow-up TSH tests every 6-12 weeks initially.

If TSH is low (hyperthyroidism):

Your doctor will order Free T4, Free T3, and radioactive iodine uptake scan to identify the cause. Treatment options include anti-thyroid medications, radioactive iodine therapy, or surgery depending on the cause and severity.

Important: Never stop or adjust thyroid medication without consulting your doctor. Both over-treatment and under-treatment carry significant health risks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Should I fast for a TSH test?

Fasting is not required for TSH. However, if you take thyroid medication, some doctors recommend taking it after the blood draw for the most accurate reading.

Q: Can stress affect TSH?

Yes. Severe physical or emotional stress can temporarily affect TSH levels. If results seem inconsistent with symptoms, a repeat test after recovery may be warranted.

Q: How often should TSH be tested?

Once diagnosed and stable on treatment: every 12 months. When adjusting medication: every 6-8 weeks until stable. Pregnant women with thyroid disease: every trimester.
